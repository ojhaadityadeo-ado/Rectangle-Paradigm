Proof Progression

1. Initial Setup Select lengths for the legs $a$ and $b$ of a right-angled triangle.
2. The Outer Square We construct a large bounding square with side length $(a+b)$. The total area of this shape is exactly $(a+b)^2$.
3. Four Right Triangles We place four identical copies of our right triangle into the corners of the square. Each has area $\frac{1}{2}ab$. Combined area is $2ab$.
4. The Inner Square The remaining empty space forms a tilted square. Its side length is precisely the hypotenuse $c$. The area of this central void is $c^2$.
5. Equating the Areas The area of the large outer square is exactly equal to the sum of its internal pieces: the 4 triangles plus the inner square.
6. Algebraic Conclusion $(a+b)^2 = 4(\frac{1}{2}ab) + c^2 \implies a^2+2ab+b^2 = 2ab+c^2 \implies a^2+b^2=c^2$.

Algebraic Derivation

1. Total Area (Direct Method)

By constructing the large outer square with side length $(a+b)$, we can calculate its total area directly by squaring the side:

$$ \text{Area} = (a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 $$

2. Total Area (Component Method)

We can calculate the exact same total area by summing up the geometric pieces inside the square: the 4 corner triangles and the inner tilted square.

$$ \text{Area} = 4 \left(\frac{1}{2}ab\right) + c^2 = 2ab + c^2 $$

Conclusion: Equating the Two

Since both methods calculate the area of the exact same outer square, we can set the two equations equal to each other:

$$ a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = 2ab + c^2 $$

Subtracting $2ab$ from both sides of the equation beautifully leaves us with the Pythagorean Theorem:

$$ a^2 + b^2 = c^2 $$
