Proof Progression

1. The Fractions We want to compare two fractions, $a/b$ and $c/d$. We represent them as shaded areas in two identical squares.
2. Slicing the Squares The left square is sliced horizontally into $b$ rows. The right square is sliced vertically into $d$ columns. They are currently hard to compare because the pieces are different shapes.
3. The Common Grid To make them comparable, we slice the left square vertically into $d$ columns, and the right square horizontally into $b$ rows. Both squares are now composed of exactly $b \times d$ tiny equal rectangles!
4. Counting the Pieces Since all tiny rectangles are identical, we just count them. The left shape has $a \times d$ shaded rectangles. The right shape has $b \times c$ shaded rectangles.
5. The Conclusion This is exactly why cross-multiplication works! Comparing $a/b$ to $c/d$ is physically identical to comparing $ad$ to $bc$.

Mathematical Formulation

1. Creating a Common Denominator

In order to compare two fractions $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$, they must share the same "base size" or denominator. We achieve this by multiplying the top and bottom of each fraction by the opposite fraction's denominator.

$$ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times d} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c \times b}{d \times b} $$

Visually, multiplying the denominators $(b \times d)$ corresponds to imposing the Common Grid on both squares, resulting in $bd$ total tiny rectangles in each whole.

2. Cross-Multiplication Rule

Once both fractions share the common denominator $bd$, we only need to compare their new numerators.

$$ \text{If } \frac{ad}{bd} > \frac{bc}{bd} \text{ , then } ad > bc \implies \frac{a}{b} > \frac{c}{d} $$

In our visual proof, $ad$ is exactly the number of shaded blocks in the left square, and $bc$ is the number of shaded blocks in the right square. The cross-multiplication "trick" is just comparing areas on a common grid!
