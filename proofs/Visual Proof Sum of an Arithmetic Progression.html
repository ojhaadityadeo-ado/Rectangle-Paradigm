The key idea is to pair the first term with the last, the second with the second-last, and so on.

How the visual proof works

Draw the progression as bars of width \(1\). The heights are \(a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ldots,\ell\). Their total area is exactly \(S_n\).
Make a second copy in reverse order. Put the tallest bar above the shortest, the next tallest above the next shortest, and so on.
Every vertical pair has the same total height. The first and last terms satisfy \(a+\ell\). The next pair also gives \((a+d)+(\ell-d)=a+\ell\), and the same happens in every column.
Two copies of the AP now form one rectangle. The rectangle has width \(n\) and height \(a+\ell\), so its area is \(n(a+\ell)\). Since this rectangle contains two copies of the original sum, \(2S_n=n(a+\ell)\).

The algebra hidden inside the rectangle

\[ \begin{aligned} S_n &= a+(a+d)+(a+2d)+\cdots+\ell,\\ S_n &= \ell+(\ell-d)+(\ell-2d)+\cdots+a. \end{aligned} \]

Adding the two rows term by term gives \(n\) identical sums:

\[ 2S_n=\underbrace{(a+\ell)+(a+\ell)+\cdots+(a+\ell)}_{n\text{ times}} =n(a+\ell). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a+\ell)} \]

Connection with the usual \(a,d,n\) formula

Because the last term of an arithmetic progression is \(\ell=a+(n-1)d\), the rectangle formula becomes:

\[ \boxed{S_n=\frac{n}{2}\left(2a+(n-1)d\right)} \]
