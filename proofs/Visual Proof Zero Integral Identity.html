Visual Proof: Identically Zero Function

A proof by contradiction demonstrating that if the integral of a continuous, non-negative function is zero, the function itself must be zero everywhere.

$$ \text{If } f(x) \ge 0 \text{ and } \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = 0, \text{ then } f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } x. $$

Proof by Contradiction

1. The Premise We are given a continuous function $f(x) \ge 0$ everywhere on $[a, b]$, and we are told its total integral $\int_a^b f(x)dx = 0$.
2. The Contradiction Hypothesis Assume for the sake of contradiction that $f(x)$ is NOT identically zero. Then there must exist some point $c$ where $f(c) > 0$. Let's plot this bump.
3. The Power of Continuity Because $f(x)$ is continuous, it cannot jump instantly back to zero. It must remain strictly greater than $\varepsilon = \frac{f(c)}{2}$ for some small interval $[c-\delta, c+\delta]$ around $c$.
4. Constructing the Rectangle Within this neighborhood interval, we can fit a solid rectangle of width $2\delta$ and height $\frac{f(c)}{2}$ completely underneath the curve.
5. The Mathematical Contradiction The area of this rectangle is $2\delta \cdot \frac{f(c)}{2} > 0$. Since $f(x) \ge 0$ everywhere else, the total integral must be $\ge$ the rectangle's area. Thus $\int f(x)dx > 0$. This contradicts our initial premise!
6. Resolution Since assuming a bump leads to a logical paradox, the bump cannot exist. The function must be perfectly flat: $f(x) = 0$ for all $x$.

Formalizing the Argument

1. Using the $\varepsilon-\delta$ Definition

The formal definition of continuity at a point $c$ states that for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that if $|x - c| < \delta$, then $|f(x) - f(c)| < \varepsilon$.

If we assume $f(c) > 0$, we can cleverly choose $\varepsilon = \frac{f(c)}{2}$. The definition then guarantees a $\delta$-neighborhood where:

$$ f(x) > f(c) - \varepsilon = \frac{f(c)}{2} $$

This formally creates the "floor" under our curve, enabling the rectangle.

2. Bounding the Integral

Integrals are monotonic, meaning if $f(x) \ge g(x)$, then $\int f(x)dx \ge \int g(x)dx$. Let $g(x)$ be our constructed rectangle (height $\frac{f(c)}{2}$ inside $[c-\delta, c+\delta]$ and $0$ elsewhere).

$$ \int_a^b f(x) dx \ge \int_a^b g(x) dx = 2\delta \cdot \frac{f(c)}{2} > 0 $$

Since $\delta > 0$ and $f(c) > 0$, the integral is strictly positive. But the theorem's premise was $\int_a^b f(x) dx = 0$. This contradiction proves $f(x)$ must be exactly zero everywhere.
