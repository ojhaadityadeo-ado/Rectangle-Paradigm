Why these cuts prove the identity

The general rectangle-area argument

Let A(r, s, n) be the total area of the cells on the diagonal k + m = n. The same construction works for every nonnegative r and s, not just the example on screen.

1. Represent each term by an area

Give column k width C(r, k) and row m height C(s, m). The cell on the selected diagonal has area C(r, k)C(s, n − k). Adding these cells gives A(r, s, n).

2. Cut every selected rectangle vertically

Pascal’s rule gives C(r, k) = C(r − 1, k) + C(r − 1, k − 1). Use those two numbers as the new widths. Each piece keeps the original height C(s, n − k).

3. Regroup without changing any area

The gold pieces form A(r − 1, s, n). For the teal pieces, write j = k − 1. Their height is C(s, (n − 1) − j), so they form A(r − 1, s, n − 1). Moving these pieces only translates them.

4. Finish with the base case and Pascal’s rule

When r = 0, the only column has width C(0, 0) = 1. Thus A(0, s, n) = C(s, n). Inductively, the two smaller areas are C(r + s − 1, n) and C(r + s − 1, n − 1). Pascal’s rule adds them to C(r + s, n).

Why the highlighted “diagonal” may look bent

“Diagonal” refers to the index equation k + m = n. The columns and rows have unequal sizes, so the selected cells need not lie along a straight geometric line. Their areas still represent the terms exactly.
