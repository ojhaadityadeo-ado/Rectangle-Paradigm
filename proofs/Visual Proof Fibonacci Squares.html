How to understand the visual proof

Start with \(F_1=1\) and \(F_2=1\). Their two unit squares form a rectangle of dimensions \(F_2\times F_3=1\times2\).
Assume the squares up to \(F_{k-1}\) already form a rectangle. Its side lengths are \(F_{k-1}\) and \(F_k\), so its area is \(F_{k-1}F_k\).
Attach the next square. The new square has side \(F_k\), so it fits perfectly along the side of the rectangle whose length is also \(F_k\). There is no gap and no overlap.
The other side grows by Fibonacci addition. It changes from \(F_{k-1}\) to \(F_{k-1}+F_k=F_{k+1}\). Therefore the new outer rectangle is \(F_k\times F_{k+1}\).

The same idea written as areas

Before adding the \(k\)-th square, the rectangle has area \(F_{k-1}F_k\). The new square contributes \(F_k^2\). Therefore:

\[ F_{k-1}F_k + F_k^2 = F_k(F_{k-1}+F_k) = F_kF_{k+1}. \]

But the left-hand side is exactly the sum of the areas of all the Fibonacci squares. Hence, after \(n\) squares:

\[ \boxed{\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^{n}F_j^2=F_nF_{n+1}} \]

What the picture is really teaching

The famous Fibonacci spiral is attractive, but it is not the proof. The proof is the repeating rectangle invariant: after the \(k\)-th square, the entire figure is always a rectangle whose consecutive side lengths are Fibonacci numbers.

Click through slowly and watch the dashed cyan rectangle. At every new step, one of its sides is exactly the side length of the square that is about to be added. That is the geometric reason the recurrence \(F_{k+1}=F_k+F_{k-1}\) appears.
