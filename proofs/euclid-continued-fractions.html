Count. Turn. Repeat.

01 · COUNT WHOLE SQUARES

The short side sets the square.

A larger square would be too tall to fit. Keep removing squares of this size until the leftover is narrower than one square. That round’s count is one continued-fraction term.

02 · TURN THE LEFTOVER

Ask the same question again.

The leftover is tall and narrow. Turn it so its long side is horizontal. Now its new short side sets a smaller square. Start a fresh count.

03 · READ THE COUNTS

A recipe for the original ratio.

The first count is the whole-number part. Each turn swaps which side is on top of the ratio, creating a “one over” step. The following counts nest inside those steps.

Why does turning give “one over”?

After the whole squares are removed, the leftover contributes a fraction: its narrow width divided by its height. Turning it makes the next long-to-short ratio the reverse—height divided by narrow width. The old fraction is therefore one divided by the new ratio.

That is why the pattern is “first count + 1 / (next count + 1 / …).” The shapes explain the nesting; no long division is needed.

Does this process always end?

These sliders use integer side lengths, so Euclid’s algorithm eventually leaves no rectangle. This gives a finite continued fraction for the original long-side-to-short-side ratio. With an irrational ratio of side lengths, there is always another smaller leftover, and the continued fraction continues indefinitely.

Each term counts one round of largest-square removals. Zoom changes the view, not the rectangle’s proportions.
