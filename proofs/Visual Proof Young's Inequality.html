Proof Progression

1. The Target Rectangle Construct a rectangle defined by an arbitrary width $a$ and height $b$. The geometric area of this target box is exactly $ab$.
2. Vertical Slices Approximate the area under the curve from $x=0$ to $x=a$ using vertical rectangles. Summing these yields an upper bound for $\int_0^a f(x) dx$.
3. Horizontal Slices Approximate the area left of the curve from $y=0$ to $y=b$ using horizontal rectangles. Summing these yields an upper bound for $\int_0^b f^{-1}(y) dy$.
4. The Union Cover Overlaying both sets of rectangles reveals a crucial geometric fact: they completely cover the $a \times b$ box! Thus, their combined area must be $\ge ab$.
5. The Infinite Limit As the number of rectangles approaches infinity ($N \to \infty$), the discrete steps smooth into exact continuous integrals, preserving the covering property.
6. The Equality Condition The redundant overlap (darkest region) vanishes entirely, making the sum perfectly equal to $ab$, if and only if the corner of the rectangle lies exactly on the curve ($b = f(a)$).

Deriving Hölder's Inequality

1. Applying the Integrals

Young's inequality is foundational for proving Hölder's inequality. Let's explicitly calculate the exact continuous areas for a specific curve $f(x) = x^{p-1}$ where $p > 1$.

$$ \int_0^a x^{p-1} dx = \frac{a^p}{p} $$

2. The Inverse Area

The inverse function gives $x$ in terms of $y$: $f^{-1}(y) = y^{\frac{1}{p-1}}$. Let's define a new number $q$ such that $\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = 1$, which implies $\frac{1}{p-1} = q-1$.

$$ \int_0^b y^{q-1} dy = \frac{b^q}{q} $$

3. The Final Synthesis

By overlaying the rectangles, we visually proved that the sum of the vertical and horizontal areas must always completely cover the target rectangle $ab$. Replacing the continuous integrals with our evaluated terms gives the classic arithmetic-geometric form of Young's Inequality:

$$ ab \le \frac{a^p}{p} + \frac{b^q}{q} $$
