$$ \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{64} + \dots = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^k = \frac{1}{3} $$

Proof Progression

1. The Unit Square We start with a complete square of area 1. We will partition it to represent our series.
2. First Term (k=1) Split the square into 4 quadrants. Shade the top-right quadrant. Its area is exactly $1/4$.
3. Second Term (k=2) Inside the empty bottom-left quadrant, repeat the process. Shade its top-right. Area is $1/16$.
4. Third Term (k=3) Recurse again into the new bottom-left sub-quadrant. Its top-right area is $1/64$.
5. The Fractal Pattern We spiral indefinitely into the bottom-left corner. Each term is exactly $1/4$ the area of the previous term.
6. The Infinite Limit By exploiting self-similarity, we can prove the total shaded area exactly fills $1/3$ of the available space.

Deriving the Algebraic Identity

1. Defining the Sum

Let $S$ equal the total shaded area of our infinite fractal construction:

$$ S = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{64} + \dots $$

Notice that the very first shaded box accounts for exactly $\frac{1}{4}$ of the total unit square.

2. The Self-Similarity Trick

Look closely at the bottom-left quadrant. It contains an identical, scaled-down copy of the entire fractal.

Because this sub-quadrant is exactly $\frac{1}{4}$ the size of the whole square, the infinite shaded area inside it must equal exactly $\frac{1}{4}S$.

$$ S = \frac{1}{4} \text{ (first box)} + \frac{1}{4}S \text{ (the rest)} $$

Conclusion: Solving for S

We now have a simple algebraic equation that perfectly models the geometry. We can solve for $S$:

$$ S - \frac{1}{4}S = \frac{1}{4} $$ $$ \frac{3}{4}S = \frac{1}{4} \implies S = \frac{1}{3} $$

This self-similarity argument beautifully proves that an infinite sum of powers of $\frac{1}{4}$ converges exactly to $\frac{1}{3}$. If you look closely at the diagram, for every shaded box, there are exactly two empty white boxes of the exact same size beside it, meaning the shaded region naturally covers 1 out of every 3 parts of the square!
