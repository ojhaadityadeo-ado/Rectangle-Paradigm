Proof Progression

1. The Rectangle Prove the theorem for rectangles whose sides are parallel to the axes. Suppose the side parallel to the x-axis has length $a$, and the y-axis side has length $b$. By simple counting, $A = ab$, $I = (a-1)(b-1)$, and $B = 2a + 2b$. The formula holds perfectly!
2. The Right Triangle This can be checked by cutting the rectangle from Step 1 exactly in half across its diagonal into two identical right triangles. Since the area halves and boundary points are shared, the additive property proves the formula holds.
3. An Arbitrary Triangle ($T$) Consider a bounding rectangle $R$ containing $T$. The region $R \setminus T$ can be neatly divided into several right triangles (and rectangles if needed).
4. Verifying via Subtraction This enables us to explicitly subtract off the outer right triangles ($\Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3$) from the bounding rectangle $R$ to verify the claimed formula for the inner triangle $T$.
5. Any Polygon To prove the theorem for any arbitrary lattice polygon, simply triangulate the polygon! Since Pick's theorem applies to all triangles and is additive across shared edges, it extends universally.

Algebraic Derivation of the Base Cases

1. The Rectangle Base Case

For a rectangle with integer vertices $(0,0)$ to $(a,b)$:

  • The interior points form a grid of size $(a-1) \times (b-1)$. So, $I = (a-1)(b-1) = ab - a - b + 1$.
  • The boundary points lay exactly on the perimeter. $B = 2a + 2b$.

Plugging these into Pick's formula:

$$I + \frac{B}{2} - 1 = (ab - a - b + 1) + (a + b) - 1 = ab = \text{Area}$$

2. Triangles via Subtraction

For an arbitrary triangle $T$ with vertices $V_1, V_2, V_3$, we place it in a bounding rectangle $R$. The remainder $R \setminus T$ is composed of right triangles (e.g., $\Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3$).

$$ R \setminus T = \Delta_1 + \Delta_2 + \Delta_3 $$

Because Pick's Theorem is proven for rectangles and right triangles, and because the geometric area is additive ($A_R = A_T + A_{\Delta_1} + A_{\Delta_2} + A_{\Delta_3}$), subtracting the right triangles mathematically proves that Pick's formula holds for the inner arbitrary triangle $T$.
