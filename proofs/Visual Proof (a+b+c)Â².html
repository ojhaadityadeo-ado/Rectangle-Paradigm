How to understand this proof:

Imagine a large square. We want the length of each side to be divided into three parts: $(a + b + c)$. Because the area of a square is its total side length multiplied by itself, the grand total area of this big square is exactly $(a + b + c)^2$.

Look at the visualization above. By extending lines where $a, b,$ and $c$ meet, we naturally slice the large square into nine smaller geometric pieces:

  • One Blue Square: Sides are $a \times a$, so area is $a^2$.
  • One Red Square: Sides are $b \times b$, so area is $b^2$.
  • One Purple Square: Sides are $c \times c$, so area is $c^2$.
  • Two Green Rectangles: Dimensions $a \times b$, contributing $2ab$.
  • Two Orange Rectangles: Dimensions $b \times c$, contributing $2bc$.
  • Two Teal Rectangles: Dimensions $a \times c$, contributing $2ac$.

Because these 9 pieces fit perfectly together like a puzzle to form the large square, their combined areas must perfectly equal the area of the large square!

$$(a+b+c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca$$
