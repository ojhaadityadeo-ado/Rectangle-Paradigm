Two Consecutive Triangular Numbers Make a Square

A staircase of \(1+2+\cdots+n\) unit squares and the next smaller staircase fit together perfectly to fill an \(n\times n\) square.

\[ \boxed{T_n+T_{n-1}=n^2} \qquad\text{where}\qquad T_n=1+2+\cdots+n \]

The complement of the \(T_n\) staircase inside an \(n\times n\) square has exactly \(1+2+\cdots+(n-1)=T_{n-1}\) cells.

How the visual proof works

Build \(T_n\) as a staircase. Put \(1\) unit square in the first row, \(2\) in the second, and continue up to \(n\) in the last row. Its area is \(T_n\).
Look at the missing part of an \(n\times n\) square. The empty cells occur in rows of sizes \(n-1,n-2,\ldots,1\).
That missing region is another triangular number. Its area is \(1+2+\cdots+(n-1)=T_{n-1}\).
Together, the two regions fill the whole square. Therefore their total area is exactly \(n^2\).

The area equation

The purple staircase contributes \(T_n\), while the cyan complement contributes \(T_{n-1}\). Since together they fill an \(n\times n\) square,

\[ \boxed{T_n+T_{n-1}=n^2} \]

Using the usual formulas \[ T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2, \qquad T_{n-1}=\frac{n(n-1)}2, \] the same identity becomes

\[ \frac{n(n+1)}2+\frac{n(n-1)}2=n^2. \]

Why this is a useful visual pattern

The square is partitioned by a staircase boundary. One side contains the first \(n\) natural numbers, and the complementary side contains the first \(n-1\). This “shape plus complement” idea reappears in many visual proofs involving sums, lattice points, and floor functions.
