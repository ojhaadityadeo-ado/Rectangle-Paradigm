Proof Progression

1. The Sample Space ($\Omega$) We define a $1 \times 1$ unit square. The total area is $1$, representing $100\%$ probability of all possible outcomes.
2. Construct Event $A$ Event $A$ is drawn as a vertical strip. Its width is exactly $P(A)$, and its height spans the full $1.0$ unit. $\text{Area}(A) = P(A) \times 1 = P(A)$.
3. Construct Event $B$ Independently, Event $B$ is a horizontal strip of width $1.0$ and height $P(B)$. $\text{Area}(B) = 1 \times P(B) = P(B)$.
4. The Intersection ($A \cap B$) Overlaying the two events forms a distinct rectangle in the bottom corner. Its bounds are entirely defined by $A$'s width and $B$'s height.
5. Area of the Intersection Because it forms a perfect rectangle, the geometric area—and therefore the probability of both occurring—is simply $\text{Width} \times \text{Height} = P(A) \cdot P(B)$.

Deriving the Algebraic Property

1. Orthogonal Events

By representing $A$ and $B$ along perpendicular axes, we force their interactions to act as independent dimensions. The width of $A$ places no constraints on the height of $B$.

$$\text{Area}(A \cap B) = \text{Width}_A \times \text{Height}_B$$

Geometrically, calculating the area of this overlapping sub-rectangle directly mimics the calculation for finding the joint probability of independent events.

2. Capturing Intuition ($P(B|A)$)

The core definition of independence is that the occurrence of $A$ does not change the probability of $B$: $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$.

Look at the vertical strip for $A$. If you restrict your entire "universe" to just strip $A$, what fraction of $A$ is occupied by $B$?

$$P(B \mid A) = \frac{\text{Area}(A \cap B)}{\text{Area}(A)} = \frac{P(A) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)} = P(B)$$

Conclusion: The Multiplication Rule

Because the vertical height fraction of $B$ remains exactly $P(B)$ whether you are looking at the entire unit square or exclusively inside the vertical strip $A$, the geometric ratio perfectly holds.

This cross-product structure is the direct visual analogue of probabilistic independence, yielding the standard multiplication rule:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$$
