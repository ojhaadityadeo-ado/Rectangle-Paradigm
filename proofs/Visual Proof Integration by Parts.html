The geometric idea before the formula

Think of \(u\) as a horizontal coordinate and \(v\) as a vertical coordinate. As \(x\) changes, the point \((u(x),v(x))\) traces a curve inside the rectangle.
Vertical slices have area \(v\,du\). A very thin vertical strip has width \(du\) and height \(v\), so its area is approximately \(v\,du\). Adding all such strips gives \(\int v\,du\).
Horizontal slices have area \(u\,dv\). A thin horizontal strip has height \(dv\) and width \(u\), so its area is approximately \(u\,dv\). Adding those strips gives \(\int u\,dv\).
The two regions exactly tile the product rectangle. Therefore their areas add to the rectangle area \(uv\). Rearranging that area equation is integration by parts.

Why the differential identity appears

There is also a local version of the same picture. If a rectangle has side lengths \(u\) and \(v\), then a tiny increase changes its area by two first-order strips:

Horizontal growth

Increasing the width by \(du\) adds a thin rectangle of area \(v\,du\).

Vertical growth

Increasing the height by \(dv\) adds a thin rectangle of area \(u\,dv\).

The tiny corner has area \(du\,dv\), which is second order and disappears in the differential limit. Thus

\[ \boxed{d(uv)=u\,dv+v\,du} \]

Integrating both sides from \(a\) to \(b\) gives

\[ [uv]_a^b=\int_a^b u\,dv+\int_a^b v\,du. \]

Moving one area to the other side gives the familiar rule:

\[ \boxed{\displaystyle \int_a^b u\,dv=[uv]_a^b-\int_a^b v\,du} \]

The concrete example shown above

In the interactive picture we choose \[ u=x,\qquad v=e^x,\qquad 0\le x\le b. \] Then \(du=dx\) and \(dv=e^x\,dx\). Integration by parts becomes

\[ \int_0^b x e^x\,dx =\left[xe^x\right]_0^b-\int_0^b e^x\,dx =be^b-(e^b-1). \]

The cyan region is exactly the integral \(\int_0^b x e^x\,dx\). The purple region is \(\int_0^b e^x\,dx\). Together they fill the rectangle of area \(be^b\).

One important mathematical note

The clean “two complementary regions” picture is easiest when \(u\) and \(v\) are increasing, as in the example above. The integration-by-parts identity itself is much more general: it follows from the product rule and remains valid whenever the usual differentiability/integrability conditions hold, even when the curve is not monotone.
