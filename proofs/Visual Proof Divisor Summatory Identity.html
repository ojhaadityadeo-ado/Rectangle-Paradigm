The Divisor Summatory Identity as One Picture Counted Two Ways

Each tile represents an ordered pair of positive integers \((a,b)\) satisfying \(ab\le N\). Count the same tiles by vertical columns and you get floor functions. Group them by their product and you get divisor counts. Since the collection of tiles never changes, the two totals must be equal.

\[ \boxed{\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\lfloor\frac{N}{k}\right\rfloor = \sum_{m=1}^{N} d(m)} \] where \(d(m)\) is the number of positive divisors of \(m\).

The visual proof

Give every ordered pair \((a,b)\) one unit tile. Keep the tile exactly when \[ ab\le N. \] So the whole staircase-like region represents all positive integer pairs whose product is at most \(N\).
Count the tiles by columns. Fix \(a=k\). Then \(kb\le N\), which means \[ b\le\frac Nk. \] Since \(b\) must be an integer, that column contains exactly \[ \left\lfloor\frac Nk\right\rfloor \] tiles.
Now count those same tiles by their product. Fix a product \(m\le N\). A tile has label \(m\) exactly when \[ ab=m. \] Choosing \(a\) is the same thing as choosing a positive divisor of \(m\), because then \(b=m/a\). Therefore there are exactly \(d(m)\) tiles whose product is \(m\).
Sum the two different groupings. Counting every tile column-by-column gives \[ \sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\lfloor\frac Nk\right\rfloor. \] Counting every tile product-by-product gives \[ \sum_{m=1}^{N}d(m). \]
They count the identical set of tiles. Therefore \[ \boxed{\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\lfloor\frac Nk\right\rfloor = \sum_{m=1}^{N}d(m)}. \]

A concrete example: \(N=12\)

Counting by columns gives

\[ \left\lfloor\frac{12}{1}\right\rfloor+ \left\lfloor\frac{12}{2}\right\rfloor+\cdots+ \left\lfloor\frac{12}{12}\right\rfloor = 12+6+4+3+2+2+1+1+1+1+1+1 =35. \]

Counting instead by products gives

\[ d(1)+d(2)+\cdots+d(12)=35. \]

Nothing mysterious has happened: both expressions are simply counting the same \(35\) ordered factor pairs \((a,b)\) with \(ab\le12\).

Why \(d(m)\) really appears

Example: \(m=12\)

The tiles satisfying \(ab=12\) are \[ (1,12),(2,6),(3,4),(4,3),(6,2),(12,1). \] There are \(6\) of them, exactly \(d(12)=6\).

Example: \(m=16\)

The tiles are \[ (1,16),(2,8),(4,4),(8,2),(16,1). \] There are \(5\), exactly \(d(16)=5\).

Ordered pairs matter here. Each divisor \(a\mid m\) produces exactly one tile \[ (a,m/a). \] This is the direct bijection between divisors and lattice tiles of product \(m\).

Bonus: the Dirichlet hyperbola shortcut

The picture is symmetric under swapping \(a\) and \(b\). Let \[ q=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor. \] Every valid pair has either \(a\le q\) or \(b\le q\). Count the left part and reflect it. The central \(q\times q\) square gets counted twice, so subtract it once:

\[ \boxed{\displaystyle \sum_{m\le N}d(m) = 2\sum_{k=1}^{q}\left\lfloor\frac Nk\right\rfloor-q^2, \qquad q=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor.} \]

The final animation step shows this symmetry directly. It is the elementary geometric idea behind the classical Dirichlet hyperbola method.
