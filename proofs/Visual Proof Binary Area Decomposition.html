Proof Progression

1. The Unit Area Start with a rectangle of area $1$. We'll decompose it to map directly to the bits of a binary fraction.
2. First Halving (Bit 1) Divide it in half. The left piece is $\frac{1}{2}$, which is exactly $0.1_2$ in binary.
3. Second Halving (Bit 2) Divide the remaining half horizontally. The top-right piece is $\frac{1}{4}$, which is $0.01_2$ in binary.
4. Successive Halving Continuing this alternating pattern yields $1/8 \ (0.001_2)$, $1/16 \ (0.0001_2)$, and $1/32 \ (0.00001_2)$.
5. Binary Area Decomposition Any binary fraction (like $0.101_2$) is simply the geometric union of the corresponding rectangular pieces. Adjust the slider to see!

Bridging Geometry and Computer Science

1. Base-2 Fractions

In base-10, decimals like $0.25$ represent powers of $\frac{1}{10}$ (e.g. $\frac{2}{10} + \frac{5}{100}$).

Binary fractions work exactly the same way, but use powers of $\frac{1}{2}$. Each digit after the binary point tells you whether to include a progressively smaller half:

$$ 0.b_1b_2b_3\dots = b_1 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) + b_2 \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + b_3 \left(\frac{1}{8}\right) \dots $$

2. Geometric Representation

This sequence perfectly mirrors the geometric action of taking a shape, slicing it in half, then slicing the remainder in half, ad infinitum.

When a computer stores a floating-point number, its mantissa is fundamentally just a checklist of which of these geometric sub-rectangles need to be glued together to approximate the true value. For example, $0.101_2$ means "give me the $\frac{1}{2}$ piece and the $\frac{1}{8}$ piece."

Conclusion: The Sum to One

If we set every bit to $1$ infinitely ($0.11111\dots_2$), the geometric pieces perfectly fill the entire unit square without overlapping.

$$ 0.11111\dots_2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \dots = 1 $$

This is the binary equivalent of $0.99999\dots_{10} = 1$ in decimal, beautifully proven by the fact that the infinite sequence of chopped rectangles physically exhausts the entire area!
