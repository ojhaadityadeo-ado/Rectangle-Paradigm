From rectangular strips to exact area

Why the demonstration works

01 · MATCH THE WIDTHS

Compare the same height.

At every height y, the width of figure A equals the width of figure B. Figure B may shift sideways as y changes, but the length of each horizontal cross-section stays the same.

02 · ADD EQUAL RECTANGLES

Use one shared partition.

Divide the common height H into N bands, each of height Δy = H/N. In each band, use its midpoint width. The matching rectangles have equal areas, so the two approximate totals are exactly equal.

03 · MAKE THE STRIPS THINNER

Pass to the limit.

As N grows, Δy tends to zero. For these smooth figures, the rectangular approximations converge to the true areas. Since the approximate totals agree for every N, their limits agree too.

AA,N=∑i=1Nw(yi)Δy=AB,N⟹AA=AB
The final equality follows as N → ∞. The width w(yᵢ) is measured at the midpoint of band i.

How these particular figures are constructed

The common height is H = 6 units. Both figures have width w(y) = 2 + sin(πy/6) for 0 ≤ y ≤ 6. Figure A is centered on one vertical line. Figure B shifts each cross-section sideways by b[0.85 sin(2πy/6) + 0.5(y/6 − 1/2)], where b is the bend slider value divided by 100.

This horizontal shift never changes a width. The two boundaries of B move together at each height, so the hypothesis of Cavalieri’s principle holds at every level, not only at the displayed strip midpoints.

Are the thick strips already the exact figures?

No. A finite rectangle uses only the midpoint width and midpoint position of its band. It may extend outside an exact boundary or miss a small region inside it. These local discrepancies shrink as the strips get thinner. The equality of the two finite sums is exact; their agreement with the curved figures is approximate until the limit.
