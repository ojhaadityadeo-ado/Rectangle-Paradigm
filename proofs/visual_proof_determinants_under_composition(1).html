Proof Progression

1. The Unit Rectangle We start with a standard $1 \times 1$ unit square (a rectangle). Its fundamental area is exactly $1$.
2. Apply Matrix $B$ Applying transformation $B$ shears and stretches the square into a parallelogram. The determinant measures exactly this area scaling: $\text{Area} = \det(B) = 4$.
3. The Equivalent Rectangle By Cavalieri's principle, we can "straighten" this parallelogram back into an axis-aligned $2 \times 2$ rectangle. The area remains completely identical ($\text{Area} = 4$).
4. Apply Matrix $A$ to the Rectangle Matrix $A$ scales any input area by $\det(A)$. Applying $A$ to our equivalent rectangle of area $4$ scales it by $2.5$. The final area is $\det(A) \times \det(B) = 10$.
5. Direct Composition $AB$ If we apply the combined matrix $AB$ directly to the original unit square, it produces a different geometric shape, but perfectly converges to the exact same area of $10$!

Mathematical Context

1. Determinant as Area Scaling

The defining geometric property of the determinant of a $2 \times 2$ matrix $M$ is that it represents the factor by which the transformation $M$ scales any 2D area.

If you take a region $S$ with area $\text{Area}(S)$, and apply matrix $M$ to every point in $S$, the new region $M(S)$ will have an area of:

$$ \text{Area}(M(S)) = |\det(M)| \times \text{Area}(S) $$

2. The Rectangle Principle

This is where the concept of a rectangle bridges the proof. After applying matrix $B$, we get a skewed parallelogram of area $\det(B)$.

Because area scaling applies uniformly to all shapes, we can conceptually replace that parallelogram with a simple rectangle of the exact same area. When we then apply matrix $A$, the operation is mathematically pristine:

$$ \det(AB) = \text{Area}(A(\text{Rectangle})) = \det(A) \times \det(B) $$
