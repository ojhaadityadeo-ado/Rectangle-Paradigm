The whole argument

Three things to notice

01 · AREA DEFINES THE LOG

Start at 1.

The area from 1 to b is log b. We break it into thin rectangles so that we can see how each piece changes shape.

02 · STRETCH AND SHRINK

The area stays the same.

Multiply every horizontal coordinate and every width by a. Divide every height by a. A point on the curve (t, 1/t) becomes (at, 1/(at)), so the reshaped area still lies under the same curve.

(aw)×ha=wh
03 · PUT THE AREAS TOGETHER

Multiplication becomes addition.

The orange area now runs from a to ab, but it still equals log b. Put the green area from 1 to a beside it. Together they fill the entire area from 1 to ab: log(ab).

Are the rectangles the exact curved area?

A finite set of rectangles is an approximation: each strip uses the curve’s height at its midpoint. Every individual rectangle preserves its area exactly during the transformation. As the strips become arbitrarily thin, their sums approach the curved areas, so those areas are equal too.

The faint orange shading shows the exact curved region. The outlined strips make the area-preserving transformation visible.

What if a or b is less than 1?

The law holds for every a > 0 and b > 0. For 0 < x < 1, define log x as the negative of the area from x to 1. This is a signed area, measured from 1 toward x.

The same transformation takes the directed interval from 1 to b into the directed interval from a to ab. It preserves signed area. Adding the signed areas from 1 to a and from a to ab gives the signed area from 1 to ab, even when some parts cancel.

If a < 1, the picture shrinks horizontally and stretches vertically. The factors still multiply to 1, so the area argument is unchanged.
