Integration Process

1. The Target Area Our goal is to find the exact area bound between the continuous curve $f(x)$, the $x$-axis, and the interval limits $[a, b]$.
2. Partition the Interval We divide the horizontal interval $[a, b]$ into $N$ equal sub-intervals. The width of each sub-interval is $\Delta x = \frac{b-a}{N}$.
3. Select Sample Points Within each sub-interval, we pick a sample point $x_i^*$ (Left, Right, or Midpoint) to determine the height $f(x_i^*)$.
4. Form the Riemann Sum We draw $N$ rectangles with width $\Delta x$ and height $f(x_i^*)$. Summing their areas gives us a solid approximation. Notice the over/under estimation errors.
5. The Infinite Limit As $N \to \infty$, the rectangles become infinitely thin. The approximation error vanishes entirely, and the sum perfectly converges to the Definite Integral!

Mathematical Formulation

1. Finite Approximation (Riemann Sum)

The area under the curve is approximated by summing the areas of $N$ rectangles. Each rectangle has a base width of $\Delta x$ and a height based on a sample point $x_i^*$:

$$ S_N = \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i^*) \Delta x \quad \text{where} \quad \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{N} $$

Depending on the chosen sample point $x_i^*$, this forms a Left, Right, or Midpoint Riemann Sum.

2. Infinite Limit (Definite Integral)

As we increase the number of rectangles ($N \to \infty$), the width of each rectangle $\Delta x \to 0$. The sum converges to a precise, single value.

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx = \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i^*) \Delta x $$

This limit is the formal definition of the Riemann Integral, represented continuously using the integral sign $\int$.
