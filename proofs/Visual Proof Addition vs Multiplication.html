Proof Progression

1. Discrete Units We start with two separate sets of discrete unit squares: a row of length $a$ and a row of length $b$.
2. Adjoining (Concatenation) To add $a + b$, we physically concatenate the rows end-to-end along a single dimension.
3. Recounting By counting the newly adjoined sequence sequentially, we arrive at the total length of $a + b$.
1. The Base Row To multiply $a \times b$, we use $a$ as our horizontal base row, and $b$ as our multiplier (the number of rows to create).
2. 2D Array (Extrusion) We stack the base row $b$ times into a new, orthogonal dimension (height), forming a 2D array.
3. The Birth of Area The total number of discrete unit squares present in this 2D lattice is exactly $a \times b$. This physical array is the fundamental definition of Area.

Bridging Discrete and Continuous Geometry

1. Addition as 1D Length

In elementary arithmetic, addition is defined as the union of disjoint sets. Geometrically, this translates to connecting two line segments end-to-end.

$$ \text{Length} = a + b $$

Because addition operates entirely within one dimension, it perfectly models concepts like distance and perimeter, but it inherently lacks the geometric depth to describe surface coverage.

2. Multiplication as 2D Area

Multiplication mathematically represents repeated addition: adding a group of size $a$ exactly $b$ times. When we map this geometrically, we stack the rows in an orthogonal direction.

$$ \text{Total Squares} = \underbrace{a + a + \dots + a}_{b \text{ times}} = a \times b $$

By extruding into the second dimension, the total count of $1 \times 1$ unit squares directly forms the physical area of the resulting boundary. This proves why continuous surface area is calculated as $\text{Width} \times \text{Height}$.
