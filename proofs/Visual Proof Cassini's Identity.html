What is happening? Compare the Fibonacci rectangle with the neighboring Fibonacci square. Split both using the Fibonacci recurrence. They contain one identical gold rectangle, so that common area can be ignored. The two leftover shapes are exactly the previous Cassini comparison, but in reverse order—so the sign flips. Repeating this eventually reaches a one-square-unit difference.

The rectangle-area proof

Name the area difference. Let \[ D_n=F_{n-1}F_{n+1}-F_n^2. \] This is “area of the Fibonacci rectangle minus area of the Fibonacci square.”
Split the rectangle using \(F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}\). The \(F_{n-1}\times F_{n+1}\) rectangle breaks into an \(F_{n-1}\times F_n\) rectangle plus an \(F_{n-1}\times F_{n-1}\) square.
Split the square using \(F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}\). The \(F_n\times F_n\) square breaks into the same \(F_{n-1}\times F_n\) rectangle plus an \(F_{n-2}\times F_n\) rectangle.
Cancel the identical pieces. What remains is \[ D_n=F_{n-1}^2-F_{n-2}F_n. \] But the right side is exactly the negative of \(D_{n-1}\). Therefore \[ \boxed{D_n=-D_{n-1}}. \]
Finish at the base case. At \(n=2\), \[ D_2=F_1F_3-F_2^2=1\cdot2-1^2=1. \] Every step flips the sign but keeps the magnitude, so \[ D_n=(-1)^n. \]

Why the sign alternates

Even \(n\)

The rectangle \(F_{n-1}\times F_{n+1}\) has one more unit of area than the square: \[ F_{n-1}F_{n+1}=F_n^2+1. \]

Odd \(n\)

The square has one more unit of area than the rectangle: \[ F_n^2=F_{n-1}F_{n+1}+1. \]

The alternating sign is not an extra algebraic trick. It comes directly from the geometry: after cancelling the common rectangle, the two remaining shapes switch roles.

The whole identity in one chain

\[ \begin{aligned} D_n &=F_{n-1}F_{n+1}-F_n^2\\ &=F_{n-1}(F_n+F_{n-1})-F_n(F_{n-1}+F_{n-2})\\ &=F_{n-1}^2-F_nF_{n-2}\\ &=-D_{n-1}. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(D_2=1\), repeated sign reversal gives

\[ \boxed{F_{n-1}F_{n+1}-F_n^2=(-1)^n}. \]

Connection with the famous “missing square” puzzle

For \(n=6\), Cassini says \[ 5\cdot13-8^2=65-64=1. \] This is the numerical source of the classic Fibonacci dissection illusion in which an \(8\times8\) square seems to rearrange into a \(5\times13\) rectangle. They cannot truly have the same area: the rectangle has exactly one extra square unit.
