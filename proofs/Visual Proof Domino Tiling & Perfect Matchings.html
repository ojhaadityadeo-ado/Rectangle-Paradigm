Proof Progression

1. The Rectangular Grid We start with a standard $N \times N$ rectangular area. It contains an even number of total unit squares.
2. Checkerboard Coloring By coloring the grid in an alternating pattern, we perfectly partition the area into two equal sets: $50\%$ Light squares and $50\%$ Dark squares.
3. Mutilating the Board We physically remove the top-left and bottom-right corners. Notice that because of the grid dimensions, these two squares are always the same color!
4. The Domino's Area A domino is a $2 \times 1$ rectangle. Because the colors alternate, no matter where you place it, it will always cover exactly 1 Light area and 1 Dark area.
5. The Impossibility If we completely tiled the board, the dominoes would cover an equal number of Light and Dark squares. But our mutilated board has unequal areas. Tiling is impossible!

Area Conservation & Perfect Matchings

1. Tracking the Total Area

A standard chessboard has $N^2$ squares (e.g., $64$). Removing two corners leaves a total area of $N^2 - 2$ squares (e.g., $62$).

Since each domino covers exactly $2$ squares of area, perfectly tiling the mutilated board would require exactly:

$$ \text{Dominoes Needed} = \frac{N^2 - 2}{2} = 31 $$

Mathematically, the total area matches up. So why does it fail? The area metric alone isn't enough; we need a parity invariant.

2. The Parity Invariant

By introducing checkerboard coloring, we assign an intrinsic property to every unit of area.

  • Every $2 \times 1$ domino strictly covers Area = $(1 \text{ Light} + 1 \text{ Dark})$.
  • Therefore, $31$ dominoes must cover exactly $31$ Light squares and $31$ Dark squares.
$$ \text{Available Area} = 32 \text{ Dark} + 30 \text{ Light} $$

Conclusion: Bipartite Graph Matching

This visual coloring trick is a profound application of Graph Theory. The grid can be viewed as a bipartite graph, where edges (dominoes) only connect nodes of opposite sets (Light and Dark squares).

$$ \text{A Perfect Matching requires } |V_{\text{Light}}| = |V_{\text{Dark}}| $$

Because mutilating the board destroys two squares of the same color, the two sets of the bipartite graph become fundamentally unbalanced. A perfect matching (a complete domino tiling) is strictly impossible.
